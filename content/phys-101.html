Imagine you're moving house and packing everything into cardboard boxes. If you don't write anything on the boxes, you'll have no idea what's inside each one. So you grab a marker and write "Kitchen", "Books", "Winter clothes".

A variable is exactly that: a labeled box inside your computer's memory. The label is the variable's name, and what's inside the box is its value.

Why do we even need variables?

A program constantly has to remember things: a player's score, a user's name, the price of an item. Without variables, the computer would have no place to keep any of it. Variables help in three ways:

  • They remember things. Like a phone's contact list: you don't memorize your friend's number, you just tap their name. The variable is the name, and the number is the value.
  • They let you change things in one place. If a shop's tax rate changes, you update it once in the box instead of hunting through hundreds of lines of code.
  • They make code readable. price * 2 makes sense at a glance. 49.99 * 2 makes you ask, "what is 49.99?"

Creating your first variable

In JavaScript, you make a box with the word let, give it a name, and put something inside with =:

let playerName = "Sam"; let score = 10;

Read it like a sentence: "Make a box called playerName and put Sam inside." The = sign here doesn't mean "equals" like in math. It means "put this into the box".

Boxes can be re-packed too. If Sam scores a point, you swap the contents:

score = 11;

That's why they're called variables: the value can vary.

The rules for naming variables

You get to choose the label, but there are some rules you must follow, or the computer will refuse to run your code:

  • No spaces. Use playerName, not player name. The computer thinks a space means a new word has started.
  • Only letters, numbers, underscores (_) and dollar signs ($). Symbols like - ! @ # are not allowed. The dash is the common trap, because the computer reads it as "minus".
  • Can't start with a number. player1 is fine, 1player is not.
  • Can't be a reserved word. Words like let, if and class already have a special job. It's like naming your shop "STOP": everyone would be confused.
  • Capital letters matter. score and Score are two completely different boxes, just like "Sam" and "sam" would be two different labels.

Good habits (not rules, but strongly recommended)

  • Use names that describe the contents. A box labeled "Stuff" is useless. userAge beats x every time.
  • Use camelCase for multiple words. Start lowercase, then capitalize each new word: totalPrice, firstName. The capitals look like the humps on a camel.
  • Be consistent. Pick one style and stick with it, so your future self can read your code easily.

Not sure if a name is allowed? Check the cheat sheet below. Notice that the bad names break exactly one of the rules above. 

✅ GOOD NAMES
playerName      (clear + camelCase)
totalPrice      (says what it holds)
score2          (numbers are fine, just not first)
_secret         (underscore is allowed)

❌ BAD NAMES
player name     (has a space)
2ndPlace        (starts with a number)
total-price     (dash is read as "minus")
let             (reserved word)

⚠️ LEGAL BUT UNHELPFUL
x               (x of what?)
stuff           (what stuff?)
